### Part 3: Scoreboard Rules

**[CLIP 14]:** Motion graphic showing the $50,000 profit stack. A small slice ($10,000 principal + $5% interest = $10,500) slides to Jake, while the massive remaining pile stays with the Carpenter.
**VO:** `[VO: Matter-of-fact, authoritative tone]` Here is how the scoreboard actually works. Jake lent you money; he did not invest in your business. **[CLIP 15]:** Clean comparison table animating onto the screen: Left side shows **JAKE = BONDHOLDER (Loan, Fixed Return, Lower Risk)**; Right side shows **YOU = STOCKHOLDER (Ownership, Unlimited Potential, Higher Risk)**.
**VO:** `[VO: Crisp, instructional delivery]` In the language of Wall Street, Jake is what we call a **bondholder**, and you are a **stockholder**. Look at the difference between the two:

- A **bond** is simply a loan. It says: _"Give me money today, and I will pay you back with interest."_ You get a fixed return with lower risk.
- A **stock** is pure ownership. It says: _"Give me money today, and you own a slice of my company."_ You get unlimited upside potential, but you carry way higher risk.

**[CLIP 20]:** 4K footage of a CEO ringing the opening bell at Nasdaq/NYSE as confetti falls, with giant kinetic text: **IPO (Initial Public Offering)**.
**VO:** `[VO: Clear, authoritative commentary]` On Wall Street, this milestone is called an **IPO—an Initial Public Offering**. It is the very first time a private company sells pieces of itself to the public, and it is the _only_ major stage where the money goes directly into the company’s pockets. Once the IPO is over, if an investor wants out, they don't ask the company for a refund—they have to sell their piece to another investor.

### Part 6: The Leverage Trap

**[CLIP 28]:** Animated tug-of-war rope between a massive crowd of **Buyers (Green)** pulling the price sky-high and a crowd of **Sellers (Red)** dragging it into the floor.
**VO:** `[VO: Punchy, direct]` Why do stock prices shoot up one day and crash the next? Strip away the fancy math, and it comes down to two words: **supply and demand**. That’s it. When there are lots of buyers fighting over a stock and barely any sellers, the price goes up. When everyone panics and tries to sell at once with nobody lining up to buy, the price craters. A stock is only ever worth whatever the next person is willing to pay for it.
