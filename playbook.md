The Wealth Gap: Idea-to-Upload YouTube Playbook
A step-by-step guide for a faceless finance motion-graphics channel: ideas, packaging, scripts, length, timing, Shorts, analytics, mistakes and a 90-day plan. 0. Read this first
I'm an AI, so I don't have a channel of my own. This playbook is built from how YouTube's systems work and from widely shared creator practice, so treat every number as a benchmark to test, not a law. Check current rules in YouTube Studio under Earn.
Nobody can honestly promise monetization in 3-4 months. What you can do is give yourself the best odds. Here is the math you are playing against:
• Early tier (limited perks): 500 subscribers, 3 public uploads in the last 90 days, and either 3,000 public watch hours in 12 months or 3M public Shorts views in 90 days.
• Full ad monetization: 1,000 subscribers, and either 4,000 public watch hours in 12 months or 10M public Shorts views in 90 days.
• 4,000 hours is 240,000 minutes. At an average view duration of 6 minutes, that is about 40,000 views across your long videos. Over 14-16 videos, that means about 2,500-3,000 views each on average, or a couple of breakouts that carry the rest.
• It is possible, but only if you publish consistently and improve packaging and retention each video. Many channels need 6-12 months. Plan for that, and treat month 4 as a bonus.
Your real goal for videos 1-3 is learning, not views. You are collecting click-through rate (CTR) and retention data so video 4 is better than video 1.

1. Decisions before video one
   Language and audience. Pick one and stay with it.
   • Global English: higher ad rates, much more competition.
   • Hindi or Hinglish for India: lower ad rates, less competition on quality motion-graphic content.
   • Don't mix languages on one channel early on. The algorithm learns who your audience is from who watches.
   Positioning (write this in one sentence). Example: The Wealth Gap explains how money, power and systems widen or close the gap between the rich and everyone else, using real numbers and real stories.
   Three content pillars.
1. Case studies: rises and collapses (a company, a scheme, a crisis). Highest story value.
1. How the system works: debt, inflation, taxes, housing, interest rates, explained visually.
1. Comparisons and data: rich vs poor, country vs country, then vs now.
   Ground rules.
   • Educate, don't advise. No buy/sell calls, no price predictions. In India, SEBI restricts investment advice and stock recommendations by unregistered people, so stay educational and confirm with a professional if you move toward anything advisory.
   • Cadence: 1 long video per week plus 2-3 Shorts. Commit for 12 weeks. A consistent small schedule beats bursts.
   • Faceless is fine, but use one consistent narrator voice and one visual identity.
1. Finding ideas
   The outlier method (your best tool)
   An outlier is a video that got far more views than its channel normally gets. It proves the topic and packaging work, not just the channel's size.
1. List 15-20 channels in your niche, including small ones (under 50k subs).
1. Sort their videos by popularity and by recent. Look for videos with 3-10x the channel's usual views.
1. Note the topic, the title pattern, the thumbnail idea and the emotion it triggers.
1. Do not copy. Ask: what is the angle nobody has done, or done badly?
   Other idea sources
   • YouTube search suggestions (type a topic and read the autocomplete).
   • Google Trends and Reddit (r/economics, r/personalfinance, r/IndiaInvestments, r/wallstreetbets for narratives).
   • Comment sections of big finance videos: questions people ask are topics.
   • News from the last 1-3 years that people only half-understand.
   • Books, annual reports, central bank and government data releases.
   Idea scorecard
   Score each idea 1-5 on each line. Only make ideas scoring 22+ out of 30.

Strong topic shapes for The Wealth Gap
• How [company or person] actually made or lost [big number]. A case study with a timeline.
• Why [common thing] costs [surprising number]. A system explainer.
• [Group A] vs [Group B]: where the money really goes. A data comparison.
• The mistake that destroyed [famous collapse]. A lesson-driven case study.
• How the rich use [tax rule, debt or structure] and why the rest can't. Explain how it works, don't tell people to copy it.
Avoid at the start
• Stock tips and predictions.
• Breaking news you can't verify.
• Topics where the data is paywalled or vague.
• Topics so huge you can't tell a story (for example, all of capitalism). 3. Package first: title and thumbnail before the script
The title and thumbnail decide whether anyone clicks. Write them before the script, then make the video deliver exactly that promise.
Title rules
• 40-60 characters so it isn't cut off on mobile.
• One clear curiosity gap: a surprising number, a contradiction, or a question.
• Specific beats vague (use a figure, a name, a time).
• Front-load the important words.
• No lies. If the video doesn't deliver, retention collapses and the algorithm stops showing it.
• Write 10 versions, pick the best 2, and test if the tool is available to you.
Patterns that work in finance:
• The [number] [thing] that [surprising result]
• Why [authority or common belief] is wrong about [topic]
• How [entity] [dramatic action] in [short time]
• What happened to [entity] after [event]
Thumbnail rules
• Max 3 elements: one focal image or number, a short text of 1-4 words, and a contrasting background.
• It must read clearly at about 120 px wide (check it on your phone).
• High contrast, big shapes, one emotion. No clutter.
• The title and thumbnail should add to each other, not repeat. If the thumbnail says COLLAPSED, the title gives the how and why.
• Reuse one consistent template (colors, font, text placement) so viewers recognize you.
• Make 2-3 variants. Use YouTube's Test & Compare in Studio when it is available to you.
Quick packaging test
Show the thumbnail and title to 3-5 people for 3 seconds and ask what the video is about and whether they'd click. If they can't answer, redo it. 4. Length, structure and retention
How long should the video be?
• 8-12 minutes for your first 10 videos. That length fits a story with 3-4 beats and qualifies for mid-roll ads (long enough, generally 8+ minutes).
• Go longer (12-20 min) only when the story truly needs it and your retention is already strong.
• Never pad. A tight 9 minutes beats a stretched 14. The algorithm rewards viewers staying and coming back, not duration for its own sake.
• Narration pace is about 140-160 words per minute. A 10-minute video is roughly 1,400-1,600 words.

Writing the hook (the first 30 seconds decides the video)
The viewer already decided to click. The hook must confirm they made the right choice, fast.
• Open mid-story or on the strangest fact. No 'Hey guys welcome back'. No channel intro.
• Confirm the title's promise in the first 10 seconds.
• Common hook shapes: a shocking stat, a mini-scene, a direct contradiction, or 'In [year], [person] did [surprising thing]. Here's how.'
• Cut anything that isn't earning its place. If the first 20 seconds could be deleted without loss, delete them.
Keeping people watching
• Use open loops: raise a question early and answer it later.
• Re-hook every 60-90 seconds ('But there was a problem...', 'Here's where it gets strange').
• Change the visual every 3-5 seconds, and change the type of visual every 20-30 seconds (chart, then map, then diagram, then number).
• Cut tangents. Every sentence should either advance the story or explain something needed for the next point.
• Use plain language. If a term needs explaining, explain it with a visual in the same breath. 5. Research and scripting

1. Gather primary sources: company filings and SEC EDGAR, central bank and government statistics, FRED, World Bank, IMF, RBI and SEBI reports, court documents, reputable news archives.
2. Build a number log (a simple spreadsheet): each figure, its source link and date. Every on-screen number must trace to it.
3. Outline the story as 10-15 beats in plain sentences before writing.
4. Write for the ear: short sentences, one idea each, spoken words not essay words. Read it aloud. If you stumble, rewrite.
5. Use two columns: narration on the left, visual idea on the right (for example, 'two bars, top 1% vs bottom 50%, top bar grows off screen').
6. Cut 15% on the second draft.
7. Fact-check pass: verify every claim about real people and companies. Use careful wording ('according to filings', 'the company reported'), and avoid accusations you can't prove.
   AI can draft, but you edit. The version viewers hear should have your angle and judgment in it. That's what separates a channel from slop.
8. Voice, visuals and editing
   Audio (more important than visuals)
   • Clean voiceover with no echo or background hiss. Bad audio causes drop-offs faster than plain visuals.
   • Final loudness around -14 LUFS (YouTube normalizes near that level).
   • Music sits well under the voice (a very quiet bed) and changes at chapter breaks. Use only licensed music.
   • Add subtle sound design (soft whooshes, ticks on numbers). Don't overdo it.
   Visual design
   • One design system: palette, 1-2 fonts, motion rules (from your DESIGN.md).
   • Show the idea, not the sentence. Avoid putting narration text on screen word for word.
   • Max about 8 words of text per scene. Big numbers. Plenty of empty space.
   • Use easing and staggered entrances, slow camera drift, and layered backgrounds.
   • Pick one shocking visual per chapter (the 'set piece') and spend extra time on it.
   Export
   • 1080p minimum. 1440p or 4K uploads often get better compression quality on YouTube.
   • 30 fps is fine for motion graphics.
   • Make sure your captions or subtitle file are accurate, and upload an SRT.
9. Upload, metadata and timing
   Upload checklist
   • Title: final, tested.
   • Thumbnail: uploaded at 1280x720 or larger, under 2 MB.
   • Description: the first 2 lines are visible before 'more', so put the hook and keywords there. Then chapter timestamps, sources, disclaimer ('For education only, not financial advice'), and links to the next video.
   • Chapters: timestamps starting at 0:00 (at least 3).
   • Tags: minor; use a few. Put the topic in the title and description instead.
   • Playlist: add to a series or pillar playlist.
   • End screen: in the last 10-20 seconds, point to your next best video.
   • Cards: link a related video at the moment the topic comes up.
   • Audience: set 'Not made for kids'.
   • Disclosures: if you use realistic altered or synthetic content, turn on the altered-content disclosure. Check the current rules for AI voices and visuals in Studio.
   • Comments: on, with held-for-review for links.
   • Upload as unlisted first and watch it once end to end on your phone to catch errors.
   When should you publish?
   Time matters less than consistency and packaging, but it helps the first hours.
   • Pick a fixed day and time and keep it, so viewers form a habit.
   • Publish roughly 1-3 hours before your audience's peak. Find it in Studio under Analytics, Audience, 'When your viewers are on YouTube'. You won't have data at first, so start with a hypothesis and adjust.
   • Starting hypotheses (test them): for an India-focused audience, early evening IST on weekdays or weekend mornings; for a US audience, late morning to afternoon ET on Thursday to Saturday.
   • Don't use Premieres at the beginning unless you have a community to join them. A normal upload is fine.
10. Shorts strategy
    • Make 2-3 Shorts per long video, 20-45 seconds each. Each one needs its own hook in the first second, not a random crop.
    • Format: one surprising fact, one visual, one payoff, and a clean loop ending.
    • Re-edit for vertical (9:16) with large text and fast cuts. Don't just crop the long video.
    • Link each Short to the full video with the 'related video' option.
    • Stagger them: for example, one on publish day, one 2 days later, one 4-5 days later.
    • Be realistic: Shorts viewers are different from long-form viewers, and Shorts subscribers often don't watch long videos. Shorts help discovery and subscriber count, but long-form is your main engine for watch hours and revenue. Shorts pay far less per view.
    • Alternative route: 3M Shorts views in 90 days qualifies for the early tier, but don't build the plan around it.
11. The first 48 hours
    On publish:
    • Reply to every comment in the first 24 hours. Pin a comment with a question that sparks discussion.
    • Share where it is genuinely useful (communities that allow it, your Shorts, your story). Never spam links, since low-quality traffic can hurt retention.
    Check at 1 hour, 6 hours, 24 hours and 48 hours: impressions, CTR, average view duration, and the retention graph.
    Early benchmarks for a new channel (guides, not rules):
    • CTR: 4-8% is healthy. Under 3% suggests a packaging problem. It naturally falls as impressions grow.
    • Average percentage viewed: 40%+ is good for a 10-minute video.
    • Early retention (the 'stayed to watch' number in Studio): aim for 70%+.

13. Common mistakes
14. Starting the script before the title and thumbnail exist.
15. Slow intros: logos, 'welcome back', long setups.
16. Reading the script off the screen instead of showing the idea.
17. Picking topics because they're trendy rather than because you have an angle and data.
18. Clickbait that the video doesn't pay off, which kills retention and trust.
19. Bad audio or flat, monotone narration.
20. Inconsistent visuals or posting schedule.
21. Copying other creators' scripts or structure too closely (it's plagiarism and risks demonetization).
22. Unverified numbers, which can destroy credibility fast in finance.
23. Giving specific investment advice.
24. Judging a video in the first 6 hours or changing everything too soon.
25. Ignoring the retention graph. It tells you exactly where you lost people.
26. Making videos too long without a story to justify it.
27. Buying subs, sub-for-sub, or fake engagement. Subscribers who never watch hurt your signals and can get you disqualified.
28. Burning out by overbuilding automation before the first video is good.
29. Unlicensed music or footage, which means copyright claims or strikes.
30. Opportunities most beginners miss
    • Search plus browse: pick topics that have some search demand and a story. Search brings steady views for years.
    • Series: a repeatable format (for example, 'Anatomy of a Collapse') builds a binge habit and lets viewers go from one video to the next.
    • Your own data visualizations: charts built from raw public data are original and hard to copy.
    • Local angles: India-specific finance stories are under-served in polished motion-graphic form.
    • Update and repackage: if a video gets impressions but a weak CTR, a new thumbnail can reset it.
    • Comments as a topic source: answer a big question from a comment in the next video.
    • Playlists and end screens: push viewers to the next video to raise session time.
    • Collaborations and communities: small channels in your niche can cross-promote without conflict.
31. Money, realistically
    • Ad revenue starts only after you join the Partner Program, and the amount depends on views, watch time and audience country. Finance usually earns more per 1,000 views than most categories, and a US audience earns several times more than an India-only one.
    • Early on, income is small. Don't plan your finances around it for the first year.
    • Later options: sponsorships, your own products or courses, and carefully disclosed affiliates. In finance, be careful that affiliate links don't compromise trust or run into advice regulations.
32. Compliance and risk
    • Music and footage: use licensed or original only (a subscription library, the YouTube Audio Library, or your own). Keep license receipts.
    • Fair use is a defense, not a safe harbor. Don't rely on it for clips of other people's videos.
    • Reused or inauthentic content: YouTube can limit monetization for mass-produced or templated content with little original value. Original research, a clear angle and your own voice and editing protect you.
    • AI disclosure: follow the current rules on disclosing realistic synthetic or altered content.
    • Defamation: state only what you can source, use careful language, and don't speculate about guilt.
    • Disclaimer line (put in every description): This video is for educational purposes only and is not financial advice. Do your own research or consult a licensed professional.
